How time zones work

The Earth rotates once every 24 hours, which means the sun reaches its highest point at a different moment for every longitude on the planet. If every town kept its own solar time, neighbouring towns would disagree by minutes and railway timetables would be impossible. The solution, agreed internationally in the late nineteenth century, was to divide the world into bands, each roughly 15 degrees of longitude wide, and have every place inside a band share the same clock time. One full rotation is 360 degrees, and 360 divided by 15 gives the 24 standard zones that anchor the system.

In practice the bands bend around national and regional borders, because a single country usually prefers a single clock. That is why the map of time zones looks jagged rather than striped, and why the real answer to how many time zones there are is more than 24. Every zone is described by its offset from a single reference time: the number of hours and minutes it sits ahead of or behind that reference. New York in winter is five hours behind it; Karachi is five hours ahead of it all year round.

That reference time is where the alphabet soup begins, so it is worth reading our full explanation of how time zones work, from the prime meridian to the International Date Line, before your next trip or cross-border project.

UTC and GMT, in plain English

GMT — Greenwich Mean Time — is the older of the two standards, based on the average solar day as observed from the Royal Observatory in Greenwich, London. UTC — Coordinated Universal Time — is its modern successor, kept by atomic clocks and adjusted with occasional leap seconds so it never drifts far from the Earth's rotation. For everyday purposes the two are interchangeable: when a zone is described as "UTC+5", it is five hours ahead of both. The differences only matter to scientists, satellite operators and people who write time zone software.

The important practical point is that UTC itself never changes for daylight saving. It is the fixed ruler against which every other zone is measured, which is why flight schedules, server logs and international contracts are so often written in it. Our article on UTC vs GMT explains where each term came from, when they genuinely differ, and which one you should use in your own documents.

Daylight saving time: the moving part

Roughly 70 countries shift their clocks forward by one hour in spring and back in autumn, a practice called daylight saving time in North America and "summer time" in Europe. The idea is to move an hour of daylight from the early morning, when most people are asleep, to the evening, when they can use it. Whether it still makes sense is debated every year, but while it exists it has one undeniable effect on conversions: the offset between two places is not constant.

New York and London are normally five hours apart. For a few weeks each spring and autumn, when one city has changed its clocks and the other has not, the gap shrinks to four hours. Places in the southern hemisphere, like Sydney, change their clocks in the opposite months, so the Sydney–London gap swings between nine and eleven hours across the year. The converter above handles all of this automatically because it uses the official rules for the exact date you select — but if you want to understand the rules themselves, read daylight saving time explained.

The best times for international calls

A workable call needs both sides awake and, ideally, inside reasonable hours — say 8:00 in the morning to 10:00 in the evening. The wider the gap between two cities, the smaller the overlap. New York and London share a comfortable window: 8:00–13:00 in New York is 13:00–18:00 in London, squarely inside both working days. London and Karachi overlap almost as kindly, because Karachi is only four or five hours ahead depending on the season.

The hard pairs are the ones separated by ten or more hours. Between New York and Sydney there is no hour that falls inside both standard working days; someone always has to take the call early or late, and fair teams rotate who that someone is. Between London and Tokyo the only comfortable overlap is early morning in London, late afternoon in Tokyo. Our guide to the best meeting times across time zones works through the most common city pairs with specific suggested hours, and the frequently asked questions page covers the etiquette of rotating inconvenient slots.

A simple habit prevents most disasters: never send a meeting time as a bare number. "Let's talk at 3" means nothing across borders; "3:00 pm your time (Karachi), 10:00 am mine (London)" means everything. Better still, send the time converted both ways and state the date in both cities, because the call may fall on different calendar days.

How Time Zones Work

TimeZoneHub guide · Reading time about 5 minutes

Every time zone on Earth exists because of one simple fact: the sun does not rise everywhere at once. As the planet turns, noon — the moment the sun sits highest in the sky — sweeps steadily westward across the surface. A town 15 degrees of longitude east of you experiences noon a full hour earlier. For most of human history that was no problem at all, because nobody travelled or communicated faster than a horse. Each town kept its own local time, set by its own sundials and church clocks, and the few minutes of difference between neighbours never mattered.

Railways forced the issue

The nineteenth century broke that arrangement. Railways needed timetables, and a timetable is meaningless when every station along the line keeps a different time. Britain standardised on a single "railway time" in 1847, taken from the Royal Observatory at Greenwich. North America's railroads followed in 1883, dividing the continent into the eastern, central, mountain and Pacific zones that survive today. In 1884 an international conference in Washington formalised the idea worldwide: the meridian through Greenwich became longitude zero, the prime meridian, and the world was notionally divided into 24 zones, each one hour apart.

Offsets: the one idea that explains everything

Modern timekeeping rests on a single reference — Coordinated Universal Time, UTC — and every zone is defined by how far it sits from it. Karachi is UTC+5: when it is noon UTC, it is 17:00 in Karachi. New York in winter is UTC−5: noon UTC is 07:00 there. Converting between any two zones is just arithmetic on their offsets: subtract the "from" offset, add the "to" offset. The reason conversions still trip people up is that offsets are not fixed properties of a place. Many zones change their offset twice a year for daylight saving time, a few use offsets of 30 or 45 minutes rather than whole hours, and governments occasionally redraw the map entirely — Samoa famously skipped a whole day in 2011 by jumping across the Date Line.

Why the lines are not straight

If zones followed longitude exactly, a single city could straddle two times. So boundaries bend around borders, rivers and communities. China, which spans five geographical zones, keeps one single time nationwide — which is why the sun can rise at 10:00 in the far west of the country. Spain keeps the same time as Germany despite lying on roughly the same longitude as Britain, a legacy of a 1940 decision that was never reversed. France, counting its overseas territories, touches more zones than any other country: twelve.

The International Date Line

Somewhere, the day has to start. The International Date Line runs through the Pacific Ocean, roughly opposite the prime meridian. Cross it westward and you jump a day forward; cross it eastward and you repeat a day. It zigzags to keep island nations on one side or the other as whole countries. The line is the reason the world clock on our home page can show Tuesday in New York and Wednesday in Sydney at the same instant — both are correct, separated by a line drawn in the ocean.

The database behind the converter

All of these rules — every offset, every historical change, every future clock shift — are recorded in the IANA time zone database, maintained by the Internet Assigned Numbers Authority and built into every modern operating system and browser. The converter on TimeZoneHub reads those rules directly through your browser, which is why it can tell you that New York and London are five hours apart in January but four hours apart for a few weeks in March. When a government announces a change, the database is updated and the converter's answers change with it — no stale tables, no guesswork.

UTC vs GMT: What Is the Difference?

TimeZoneHub guide · Reading time about 4 minutes

Ask a room of people what time it is "in GMT" and someone will eventually ask whether you meant UTC. The two abbreviations are used interchangeably in everyday conversation, on flight tickets and in meeting invitations — and for almost every practical purpose, that is perfectly fine. But they are not the same standard, they were created a century apart for different reasons, and in a handful of technical fields the difference genuinely matters.

GMT: time from the sun

Greenwich Mean Time is the older standard. It was defined by astronomers at the Royal Observatory in Greenwich, London, as the average — the "mean" — of the solar day: the time between one noon and the next, smoothed over the year to iron out the Earth's slightly uneven motion. Britain adopted it as its legal time in 1880, and the 1884 prime meridian conference made Greenwich the world's zero longitude, so GMT became the reference from which all other zones were measured. For nearly a hundred years, if you wanted to know the world's time, you ultimately asked what the clocks at Greenwich said.

UTC: time from atoms

By the 1960s, atomic clocks could measure time far more precisely than telescopes could, and scientists wanted a standard that did not depend on the Earth's wobbling rotation. The result was Coordinated Universal Time, UTC — defined by atomic time but kept in step with the Earth's rotation by inserting an occasional leap second. UTC was adopted internationally in 1972 and is now maintained by the Bureau International des Poids et Mesures, which combines readings from hundreds of atomic clocks in laboratories around the world. Every time zone offset you have ever seen — UTC+5, UTC−8 — is measured against it.

Where they actually differ

Three differences matter. First, precision: GMT, tied to the rotating Earth, can drift by fractions of a second against atomic time, while UTC is exact by definition — critical for GPS satellites, financial trading systems and scientific measurements. Second, daylight saving: GMT as a named zone is what the United Kingdom uses in winter, switching to British Summer Time (BST) in summer; UTC never changes, which is why it is the safe choice in international documents. Third, usage: GMT survives as a zone name in the IANA database and in everyday British speech, while UTC is the standard in aviation, computing, weather forecasting and space flight. The BBC World Service, a famous holdout, still announces GMT.

Which one should you use?

In casual conversation, either — the person you are talking to will understand both as "the time at zero offset". In anything written down that matters, prefer UTC with an explicit offset: "14:00 UTC" or "19:00 (UTC+5)" cannot be misunderstood in any country or season. The one real trap is writing "GMT" in summer about a British time: London in July is on BST, one hour ahead of GMT, and that single hour has derailed many meetings. The TimeZoneHub converter sidesteps the whole question by working with named places and dates rather than abbreviations.

Daylight Saving Time Explained

TimeZoneHub guide · Reading time about 5 minutes

Twice a year, in roughly 70 countries, hundreds of millions of people change their clocks — forward one hour in spring, back one hour in autumn. The practice is called daylight saving time (DST) in North America and Australia, and summer time in Europe. It is loved, hated, debated in parliaments every year, and responsible for a large share of all time zone confusion. This article explains what it is, why it exists, and exactly what it does to your conversions.

The basic idea

In summer, the sun rises early — often before most people wake. That early daylight is, in a sense, wasted. Daylight saving moves one hour of it to the evening: clocks jump forward, so the clock says 19:00 when the sun is where it would be at 18:00. Evenings feel longer and lighter; mornings start darker. In autumn the hour is handed back. The mnemonics are "spring forward, fall back". Nothing about the day itself changes — there are still 24 hours of rotation — only the labels on the hours shift.

A short history

The idea was floated half-jokingly by Benjamin Franklin in 1784 (he suggested Parisians could save candles by waking earlier) and campaigned for seriously by the entomologist George Hudson, who wanted lighter evenings to collect insects. Germany was the first country to adopt it nationally, in 1916, to save coal during the First World War; Britain and the United States followed. It was dropped in peacetime, revived in the Second World War, expanded during the 1970s energy crisis, and has been expanding and contracting by legislation ever since. The European Union voted in 2019 to abolish the seasonal change, though the change has not yet been implemented; the United States Senate passed a similar bill in 2022 that did not become law.

Who observes it, and who does not

Most of Europe, North America, and parts of South America, Australia and New Zealand observe some form of it. Most of Asia and Africa do not — including Pakistan, India, China and Japan — and several countries that once did, such as Russia and Turkey, have stopped, choosing to stay on their summer offset permanently. Crucially, the southern hemisphere runs on the opposite calendar: Sydney springs forward in October and falls back in April, exactly out of phase with London and New York.

What it does to conversions

Because changeover dates differ between countries, offsets between two cities are not constant. New York and London are five hours apart for most of the year, but the United States changes its clocks two to three weeks before Europe in spring, and a week later in autumn — so for those in-between weeks the gap is four hours. London and Sydney swing between nine and eleven hours apart across the year because their changes run in opposite directions. Any conversion tool that uses a fixed offset table will be wrong for part of the year. The TimeZoneHub converter uses the official IANA rules for your exact date, which is why converting the same clock time in January and July can honestly produce different answers.

Practical tips

  • Be extra careful booking calls in March, April, October and November — the changeover months.
  • Remember that the clocks change at 2:00 or 3:00 in the morning locally, so a red-eye flight can land in a different offset regime than the one it left.
  • When a time absolutely must not be misunderstood — a product launch, a filing deadline — state it in UTC as well. UTC never observes daylight saving, as explained in UTC vs GMT.
  • If you run a recurring international meeting, re-check the converted time after every changeover; calendar software usually handles it, but recurring invites created by hand often do not.

How Many Time Zones Are There in the World?

TimeZoneHub guide · Reading time about 4 minutes

It sounds like a trivia question with one clean answer. It is not. Depending on what you choose to count — theoretical bands, distinct UTC offsets, countries' official zones, or entries in the database your computer uses — you can defend answers of 24, 38, or more than 400. Here is why each number exists, and which one you actually need.

24: the textbook answer

The Earth rotates 360 degrees in 24 hours, so each hour of time corresponds to 15 degrees of longitude. Divide the globe into 24 bands of 15 degrees and you get 24 time zones, one for each hour of the day. This is the system sketched at the 1884 prime meridian conference and printed in every school atlas. It is a useful mental model — and almost nowhere on land do the real boundaries follow it, because, as how time zones work explains, borders bend for politics and convenience.

38: the offsets actually in use

Count the distinct offsets from UTC that real places currently use, and the total climbs to about 38. The extras come from three sources. First, half-hour zones: India (UTC+5:30), Iran (UTC+3:30) and parts of Australia refuse to round to the nearest hour. Second, 45-minute zones: Nepal keeps UTC+5:45, reportedly to assert independence from India's time, and the Chatham Islands of New Zealand use UTC+12:45. Third, daylight saving splits single zones into pairs — a place at UTC+1 in winter and UTC+2 in summer contributes two offsets across the year. The extremes of the system are UTC−12 (uninhabited US Pacific territories) and UTC+14 (the Line Islands of Kiribati), meaning that at one moment each day, three different calendar dates exist simultaneously somewhere on Earth.

400+: the database answer

Your phone and computer do not store offsets; they store named zones from the IANA time zone database, such as America/New_York or Asia/Karachi. There are several hundred of these, because the database records history as well as geography: two cities in the same country need separate entries if their rules ever differed in the past. Indiana in the United States alone has several entries thanks to its famously complicated local history with daylight saving. When you open the converter on TimeZoneHub, the long list in the dropdowns is exactly this database, served by your browser.

And by country?

France leads the world with 12 time zones, thanks to overseas territories from the Caribbean to the Pacific — though metropolitan France itself uses just one. The United States and Russia each span 11 by the same counting method. At the other extreme, China uses a single zone (UTC+8) across a landmass that would naturally cover five, and India uses one (UTC+5:30) across two. The practical lesson for travellers and remote teams: never assume a country has "a" time zone until you have checked the specific city.

So — 24 in theory, about 38 offsets in practice, several hundred named zones in the database. All three answers are correct; they are just counting different things. For the reference all of them are measured against, read UTC vs GMT.

Best Meeting Times Across Time Zones

TimeZoneHub guide · Reading time about 5 minutes

Every international team eventually discovers the same truth: there is no universally good meeting time, only fairly shared ones. But "fair" still leaves a wide range of better and worse options for each pair of cities. This guide works through the most common pairings with concrete hours, using a simple standard — a good slot keeps both sides between 8:00 in the morning and 10:00 in the evening, and a great slot keeps both inside the working day.

New York and London (5 hours apart, 4 in changeover weeks)

The friendliest long-distance pairing in business. Schedule between 8:00 and 13:00 in New York, which is 13:00 to 18:00 in London. Late morning in New York — 11:00, or 16:00 in London — is the sweet spot: both sides are alert, and neither is at the edge of their day. Beware the March and October changeover weeks described in daylight saving time explained, when the gap temporarily narrows to four hours.

London and Karachi (4–5 hours apart)

Karachi does not observe daylight saving, so the gap is five hours in British winter and four in summer. The comfortable window is 10:00 to 15:00 in London — 14:00/15:00 to 19:00/20:00 in Karachi. A noon London call lands at 16:00 or 17:00 in Karachi, late afternoon, which most people find entirely reasonable. This is one of the easiest intercontinental pairings to run a daily stand-up on.

London and Dubai (3–4 hours apart)

Dubai is four hours ahead of London in winter and three in summer. Almost the whole London working day overlaps Dubai's: 9:00 in London is 12:00 or 13:00 in Dubai. The only trap is the weekend — Friday is part of the weekend in the UAE's public sector rhythm and a holy day, so keep recurring meetings Monday to Thursday.

London and Tokyo (8–9 hours apart)

Now it gets tight. The only overlap inside both reasonable days is early in London and late in Tokyo: 8:00–10:00 in London is 16:00–19:00 in Tokyo (17:00–20:00 in British summer). There is no slot that puts both sides mid-morning, so fix the London-early/Tokyo-late pattern and protect it rather than rotating — rotation here just makes both slots bad half the time.

New York and Sydney (14–16 hours apart)

The hardest common pairing. Sydney is effectively on the other side of the clock: when it is 9:00 Monday in New York, it is already midnight or later in Sydney. The workable compromise is the hinge of the day — 17:00–19:00 in New York is 7:00–9:00 the next morning in Sydney. New York takes the late slot, Sydney takes the early one, and the meeting is short. Verify the exact day shift with the converter every time, because this pairing crosses the Date Line and the gap swings by two hours across the year.

Rules that help every pairing

  • Rotate the pain. If a slot is genuinely bad for one side, alternate who takes it, month by month. Nothing corrodes a team faster than one office permanently owning the midnight call.
  • Write both times, both dates. "Tuesday 17:00 New York / Wednesday 08:00 Sydney" cannot be misread; "Tuesday 5 pm" can, and will be.
  • Recheck after clock changes. A recurring slot that was perfect in February can drift an hour in March. The rules behind the drift are in how time zones work.
  • Default to recording. For gaps over ten hours, an async video update plus a short written summary often beats any live slot you can find.

Frequently Asked Questions

Short answers to the questions people ask most about time zones and conversions. For the long versions, see the articles.

How does the TimeZoneHub converter work?

It reads the official IANA time zone rules built into your browser, works out the exact UTC instant for the date and time you enter in the "from" zone, then expresses that same instant in the "to" zone. Because it uses the real rules for your exact date, daylight saving changes are handled automatically.

Why is the time difference between two cities not the same all year?

Because of daylight saving time. If one city changes its clocks and the other does not — or they change on different dates, or in opposite seasons — the gap between them grows or shrinks by an hour. See daylight saving time explained.

What is UTC, in one sentence?

UTC is the world's reference time, kept by atomic clocks, from which every time zone's offset is measured; it never changes for daylight saving. The longer story is in UTC vs GMT.

Is GMT the same as UK time?

Only in winter. The United Kingdom uses GMT in the winter months and British Summer Time (BST, one hour ahead) from late March to late October. A meeting set for "3 pm GMT" in July is genuinely ambiguous — one more reason to convert by city name instead.

Which country has the most time zones?

France, with 12, counting its overseas territories. The United States and Russia follow with 11 each. China, despite its size, uses just one. Details in how many time zones are there?

Why do some zones use half hours or 45 minutes?

Because their standard time was set by local solar time or by political choice rather than by rounding to the nearest hour. India is UTC+5:30, Nepal is UTC+5:45, and Iran is UTC+3:30. The converter handles these exactly like whole-hour zones.

What is the biggest possible time difference between two places?

26 hours, between UTC−12 and UTC+14. In practice, the largest gap between two major cities is the 16 hours that can separate New York and Sydney in some months — which is why that pairing has no comfortable meeting slot, as covered in best meeting times across time zones.

Does TimeZoneHub store the times I convert?

No. Every conversion happens entirely in your browser; nothing you enter is sent anywhere or saved. See the privacy policy for the full statement.
